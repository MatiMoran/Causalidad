One last thing.
Can we have bias due to self-selection in randomized experiments?
For example, consider a randomized trial of Aspirin and stroke.
Eligible individuals are assigned to either Aspirin, A equals 1,
or to no Aspirin, A equals 0, and then followed for five years.
However, only individuals who consent to participate in the trial
would be included in it.
The study's conditional on consent at baseline.
By definition, self-selection is present in any human experiment, at least
if we conduct it under reasonable ethical standards.
If we represent consent by C on the causal graph,
do we expect bias due to conditioning on this form of self-selection?
The answer is no.
In a randomized trial, the decision to consent
is made before the treatment is assigned.
That is, the assignment to treatment A cannot influence self-selection C,
and therefore bias under the null due to self-selection at baseline cannot
happen in a randomized trial.
On the other hand, it is possible that eligible individuals
who consent to participate in the trial are
different from eligible individuals who do not consent.
For example, eligible individuals who consent
may be less sick than the others.
And it is also possible that the effect of Aspirin, A,
on a stroke, Y, in self-selected individuals
defers from the effect in the entire population of eligible individuals.
If that is the case, then the association between A and Y
in those who consent will not be an unbiased estimate of the effect of A
and Y in the entire population of eligible individuals.
We say that the effect estimate from the randomized trial
is not transportable to populations of individuals with other characteristics.
But this situation does not create selection bias if the effect of A and Y
is null in all eligible individuals.
This is a different problem, and this is a situation
that is sometimes discussed in terms of the concepts of internal validity
and external validity.
We say that a study has internal validity when
the estimated association has a causal interpretation in the study population.
That is, there is no selection bias, there is no confounding, et cetera.
We say that a study has external validity when
the estimated association has a causal interpretation in another population.
That is, we can transport or generalize the estimated effect
to other populations.
In randomized trials, self-selection bias does not affect internal validity,
but may affect external validity.
