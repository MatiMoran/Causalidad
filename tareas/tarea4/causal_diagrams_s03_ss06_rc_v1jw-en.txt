MIGUEL HERNAN: We have discussed several forms of selection bias.
When we make casual inferences, we will need
to control for those selection biases.
Otherwise, we could incorrectly interpret an association
between treatment and outcome as an indication
of a causal effect of treatment, even if treatment had no effect on the outcome.
In this segment, we will talk about some approaches
to control for or adjust for selection bias.
But as we have said before, this is not a course about the study design or data
analysis.
So we will only outline those approaches.
OK.
First of all, an obvious way to eliminate selection bias
is to eliminate selection.
But that is not possible, because selection is necessary in all studies.
We need to select the individuals or the study
units that will be part of our study.
For example, case control designs require
selection of cases and controls.
We can use our subject matter knowledge, though,
to reduce the possibility that the selection induces bias.
For example, by selecting controls independently of treatment.
More generally, we want to avoid selecting
on colliders or their descendants, because that
is the sort of selection that may create bias even under the null.
But deciding who is selected into the study is not always possible.
For example, individuals in a follow-up study may decide to drop out
of a study, and we cannot force them to stay.
In these cases, we will need to adjust for the selection bias,
and same as in the previous lesson, when we discussed the need
to adjust for confounding.
However, there is a fundamental difference
between adjustment for confounding and adjustment for selection bias.
To adjust for confounding or better, to eliminate confounding,
we can often randomize treatment assignment.
That is, if we flip a coin to assign treatment, we expect no confounding.
That is why we said that randomization, when feasible,
is our best weapon against confounding.
But randomization of treatment assignment
does not help eliminate selection bias.
For example, we have seen in this lesson that the randomized trials
can be affected by selection bias due to defense and loss of follow-up.
For randomization to be upheld again selection bias,
we will need to randomize selection itself.
Unfortunately, this is often impossible.
We cannot randomly assign who is going to be lost to follow up.
In fact, if we could assign the variable "lost to follow up: yes or no,"
we would assign "no," lost to follow up, to everybody.
Therefore in most cases, we will need to explicitly adjust for selection bias.
Remember when we discussed confounding adjustment,
we used the backdoor criterion to learn whether we could eliminate
all bias based on the variables that we have measured,
and to learn what the measured variables are needed for bias adjustment.
Well we can also use the backdoor criterion for selection bias.
The difference is that now, we will consider
the backdoor paths between selection C and the outcome Y
rather than the backdoor paths between treatment A
the outcome Y. For example, consider the casual graph
that we discussed for selection bias due to defense and lost to follow up.
In this causal DAG, A is the treatment, Y is the outcome,
C is selection by lost to follow up or censoring,
U is the unmeasured health status that affects both censoring and outcome,
and also unmeasured health status U affects
censoring through symptoms of the disease
L, which is a measured variable.
As we discussed, there is selection bias,
because conditioning on the collider C opens a path between A and Y.
Note that the selection bias will disappear if we could block the path
from collider C to the outcome Y. That is the path C-L-U-Y.
One simple way of blocking the path is conditioning or stratifying
on the measure of variable L. Graphically,
we would put a box around L, which blocks the path C-L-U-Y and therefore
eliminates the selection bias.
This is the adjustment method that we refer to as stratification
in the previous lesson.
And there are different ways in which we can implement stratification.
For example, we can restrict the analysis
to a subset of a static population with a particular value of L. A stratum
of L, say, people without symptoms.
Or we could do the analysis separately in each stratum of L
and then pool the effect estimates.
A more sophisticated way of doing this that many of you may be familiar with
is regression analysis.
In order to use stratification to adjust for selection bias,
we need data on all the variables that are
required to block all the backdoor paths between the collider and the outcome.
In our causal graph, we need data on either L or U.
We cannot stratify on U because that variable is unmeasured,
but fortunately we can stratify on L because that variable is measured.
However, stratification doesn't always work.
Suppose that the effect of A on C is mediated by L.
There is an arrow from A to L in addition to an arrow from L to C
and therefore L is a collider on the path A-L-U-Y.
Now conditioning or stratifying on L will open the path A-L-U-Y and will
induce selection bias even under the null.
That is, stratification cannot solve our problem.
An association between A and Y will be found whether we condition on L
or we don't condition on L, even if A had no effect on Y.
Fortunately, we have another adjustment method that doesn't have this problem.
Inverse probability weighting.
IP weighting.
To use this method we first estimate for each individual
the probability of remaining under follow up, the probability of remaining
uncensored.
This probability will be conditional on treatment A and the measure
of covariate L, and therefore will be different for individuals
with different values of A and L. Then we compute the inverse
of that probability for each person.
That is, 1 divided by the probability.
And this is the IP weight for each person.
Finally, we compute the association between treatment A and outcome Y.
But we make sure that in the analysis, each person is counted as many times
as her IP weight indicates.
As an example, if a person with symptoms L equals 0 who receives therapy
A equals 1 had a 10% chance of not being lost to follow up,
then her probability of treatment is 0.1.
And her IP weight is 1/0.1 which is 10.
This person will have a weight of 10 in the analysis.
It turns out that IP weighting mathematically
eliminates the back doors into C, and it can therefore
be used to adjust for selection bias under all four
structures we have discussed.
For each of them, the arrows into C are cut by IP weighting,
which eliminates the selection bias.
Of course, a successful use of IP weighting to adjust for selection bias
requires two things.
First, knowledge about the true casual DAG.
If we don't know the true casual DAG, then we
don't know the paths that we need to block,
and therefore we don't know which variables we need to adjust for.
Second, data on the variables that need to be adjusted for.
If those data are not available, then IP weighting will not eliminate all bias,
and the magnitude of the bias will depend on how much of the backdoor
paths remain open.
