MIGUEL HERNAN: Let's go back to the problem
we discussed at the start of this lesson:
the findings on follow-up studies on smoking and dementia vary dramatically.
These are all studies that selected smokers and nonsmokers at some time
during their lifetimes and followed them until they developed dementia or died.
Yet, some studies found a higher risk of dementia in smokers than in nonsmokers.
And other studies found the opposite.
Specifically, the ratio of the rate of dementia in smokers and nonsmokers,
the Rate Ratio, range from about 0.4 to 1.4.
From a 60% lower risk of dementia in smokers
to a 40% higher risk of dementia in smokers compared with nonsmokers.
The results were even more extreme for Alzheimer's disease,
a common type of dementia.
Here, the Rate Ratio ranged from about 0.3 to 2.7,
from a 70% lower risk of Alzheimer's disease in smokers
to a 170% higher risk of Alzheimer's disease
in smokers compared with nonsmokers.
Why?
These discrepant results cannot be fully explained by chance or by variations
in confounding control across studies.
Could they be explained by selection bias?
Certainly, these studies don't have the same selection criteria.
For example, the participants in some studies
are older than in other studies.
The minimum age at baseline ranges from 55 to 75 years across studies.
Could this age difference have something to do
with the difference in the estimates?
Actually, the answer is yes.
The Rate Ratio of dementia drops from 1.4
for studies with a minimum age of 55 to 64 years, to 1.3 for 65 to 74 years,
to 0.7 for 75 years or more.
That is, from a 40% higher risk of dementia
in smokers to a 30% lower risk of dementia
in smokers compared with nonsmokers.
And the Rate Ratio of Alzheimer's disease
drops from 1.7 for studies for 55 to 64 years, to 1.2 for 65 to 74 years,
and 2.5 for 75 or more years.
That is from a 70% higher risk of dementia
in smokers to a 50% lower risk of dementia
in smokers compared with nonsmokers.
To see what is going on, we can draw a causal graph.
If smoking is represented by the variable A and dementia
by the variable Y, the arrow from A to Y represents a possible causal effect
of smoking on dementia.
In the studies with younger people, smoking
appears to increase the risk of dementia.
Now, smoking also affects the risk of death.
The variable C represents death.
And the variable U represents all the causes shared by death and dementia,
for example, risk factors for cardiovascular disease.
Does this structure look familiar?
The studies that select persons aged 75 years or older
are conditioning on having survived at least until age 75 years.
That is, they're conditioning on a particular value of the collider C.
And that conditioning induces selection bias
that appears to result in a very strong inverse association between smoking
and dementia.
That is, the effect of smoking is probably harmful overall,
but looks beneficial at older ages because of selection bias.
Another way of thinking about this, most smokers
who are susceptible to developing dementia do so by age 75.
Therefore, the group of 75-year-olds without dementia
is depleted of susceptible smokers.
In this older age groups, being a smoker is
a marker of being relatively immune to the effects of cigarette smoking
on dementia.
In this example, C represents censoring by death.
And death is referred to as a competing event for dementia.
Competing, because once a person dies we will never
know whether she was going to develop dementia in the future.
The structure of selection bias due to competing risk
is the same as the structure for selection bias due to differential loss
to follow up.
And in fact, this form of selection bias is everywhere.
The causal graph for smoking and dementia in older people that we just

And we will see more examples of bias that
