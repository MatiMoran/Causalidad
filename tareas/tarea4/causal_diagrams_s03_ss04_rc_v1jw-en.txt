MIGUEL HERNAN: OK, we now turn our attention to follow-up studies.
These are studies in which the individuals or the study units
receive different treatment values and then
are followed over time until their outcomes are measured.
For example, consider a follow-up study of HIV positive individuals.
Each individual is treated with either a new treatment, A equals 1,
or with a standard treatment, A equals 0, at baseline.
And then after one year of follow-up, the investigators
determine whether the participants have developed AIDS, which is our outcome Y.
In this follow-up study, we may want to estimate the causal effect of treatment
A on the outcome Y after one year.
In every follow-up study, we can have selection bias
even under the null for two reasons.
First, selection bias may arise because of the criteria used
to decide who is initially included in the study, who
is selected into the study.
And second, selection bias may arise because individuals
who met those eligibility criteria at baseline, then
got lost during the study.
In these two DAGs that you are seeing now, C represents selection.
In this segment, we are going to explore the selection bias that happens even
under the null when the individuals get lost after having
been selected in the first place.
In the next segment, we will talk about selection bias at baseline.
So in many follow-up studies, some individuals are lost to follow-up.
For example, in our study to estimate the causal effect
of antiretroviral therapy A on the risk of AIDS Y,
some individuals missed the follow-up visit at one year.
And that is a problem, because the outcome was supposed
to be measured at that time, and the value
of the outcome for individuals who missed the follow-up visit is unknown.
Technically, we say that the outcome Y is censored --
the censoring by lost follow-up, which we represent by the variable C.
Individuals with a censored outcome need to be excluded from the analysis,
because we cannot estimate the association between A and Y unless we
know the values of both A and Y. Therefore,
missing the follow-up visit results in selection.
The analysis needs to be necessarily restricted to individuals
who didn't miss the follow-up visit.
The question is, does this selection induce bias under the null?
Well, if the loss to follow-up happened at random, then
it wouldn't result in any selection bias.
But what if the loss to follow-up depends
on predictors of treatment and outcome?
For example, suppose individuals tend to miss the follow-up visit
when they are sick.
And we use the variable U to represent health status, how sick people are.
Then we would add an arrow from U to C. Now, because health status can also
affect the risk of AIDS, of the outcome, we also add an arrow from U to Y.
And by the way, here, and in the remainder of this discussion,
we have omitted the possible arrow from A
to Y, because we are focusing on bias under the null.
OK, let's keep building our causal graph.
The reason why sick patients missed their visit
is that they develop symptoms of the disease.
Therefore, we add the variable L, symptoms, to the causal graph.
And L is a mediator between U and C.
Now, suppose that individuals are also more likely to miss a follow-up visit
if they receive the new treatment --
say, because the treatment has side effects that make
individuals unhappy with the study.
We therefore add an arrow from A to C.
And now we say there is a differential loss to follow-up between those
with A equals 1 and A equals 0.
Look at the causal graph.
Selecting those who are not lost to follow-up, C equals 0,
is the same as conditioning on the collider
C. And conditioning on the collider C opens the path, ACLUY.
So the association flows between A and Y, now.
We will have an association between the causes of C, A, and U,
because we are conditioning on C. In other words,
differential loss to follow-up can create the selection bias.
And this bias can have several structures.
For example, suppose that both treatment A and health status
U have a causal effect on disease symptoms L,
which we represent by adding arrows from A to L and from U to L. Suppose
also that patients with symptoms are more
likely to drop out of the study, which we represent
by adding an arrow from L to C.
Now, C is not a collider.
It is the effect of the collider L, which
means that C is a common effect of A and U.
And as we discussed in lesson 1, conditioning
on a common effect C opens the path A, L, U through the collider L. Therefore,
we have selection bias, because A and Y are associated even under the null.
Something to note in these two examples is
that the differential loss to follow-up would
create selection bias even if the treatment A had been randomly assigned.
Randomization may be our best weapon against confounding at baseline,
but it is powerless against biases like these selection
bias that happen after baseline.
So we will have to worry about selection bias in randomized experiments.
These two causal graphs represent causal structures, in which the selection
bias is caused by conditioning on a common effect C of treatment A,
and a cause U of the outcome Y.
Differential loss to follow-up may induce selection bias in other ways,
too.
For example, suppose individuals with a certain personality
are more likely to seek new treatments and also less likely
to drop out of study.
We represent this personality variable by W,
and we add arrows from W to A and from W to C.
And then this structure, conditioning on C
opens the path that goes from A to Y. The path is AWCLUY.
Similarly, if the effects of health status U and personality W
were fully mediated by symptoms L, then conditioning on C,
and effect of the collider L, would open the path AWLUY, which
would create selection bias, again.
OK, so now, we have discussed four representative examples
of selection bias due to differential loss to follow-up.
We can use these four graphs to represent selection bias
due to other forms of missing data, too.
We just need to redefine censoring C as a different type of missing data.
And of course, we can propose many variations of these four graphs
to capture other causal stories.
All these examples illustrate the fact that selection bias under the null
is caused by conditioning on a variable that
is a common effect of the treatment, or a cause
of the treatment, and of the outcome, or a cause of the outcome.
