MIGUEL HERNAN: There's no selection bias without selection.
And selection is, of course, present in all studies.
But for selection to cause bias under the null,
it needs to be related to both treatment A and outcome Y.
For example, in the causal graph with arrows from A and Y to the collider C,
there is selection bias, if we condition on both individuals or study units with
certain values of C --
that is, if we only select into the analysis individuals
with certain values of C. In this case, the bias
requires the presence of an arrow from A to C, an arrow from Y to C,
and the fact that we are conditioning on C.
Some study designs always include an arrow from Y
to C, because under these designs, the study subjects
are selected according to the value of their outcomes.
Therefore, when we use these studies with outcome-based selection,
the possibility of selection bias is especially worrisome,
because the arrow from Y to C is already present, and we are conditioning on C.
Therefore, we only need an additional arrow from A
to C for the bias to arise.
Probably the most widely-used of these designs with outcome-based selection
is the case-control design.
Case-control studies are often used by epidemiologists
to estimate causal effects of A and Y. They
do so when very large studies are not possible or are not realistic.
For example, suppose we want to confirm that hormone therapy
A has no effect on lung cancer Y in women after menopause.
So we have a causal diagram with the variables A and Y. Ideally,
we would identify a large population of women--
say, all women who live in Boston.
We classify them into two groups--
those who start hormone therapy--
A equals 1-- and those who don't--
A equals 0-- and then follow them for 20 years to find out whether the groups
have a different risk of cancer--
Y equals 1.
For simplicity, let's just say that there is no confounding here.
The problem with this study design--
this follow-up study-- is that it may not be feasible,
because we don't have the means, the money,
the time to enroll all women in Boston in our study
and follow them for 20 years.
Rather, we can sample some of these women using an outcome-based selection
design.
And that is what a case-control study is.
To conduct a case-control study, first, we
select all women in Boston who have had lung cancer in the last 20 years.
Those are the cases--
Y equals 1.
Or maybe we select just a random sample of 1,000 cases
rather than all the cases.
Then we select a random sample of 1,000 women in Boston
without lung cancer at different times during the period.
These are the, quote, controls--
the non-cases-- with Y equals 0.
And that is a case-control study.
Why do people conduct case-control studies?
Because they allow us to estimate the facts in a very efficient way.
It turns out that if the relative effect of A equals 1 versus A equals 0--
hormone therapy versus no hormone therapy--
can be estimated without bias, using the data
from the entire population of women in Boston,
then it can be mathematically shown that the relative effect can also
be estimated using only the case-control data.
The precision of the estimate would be a little bit smaller.
But bear in mind that we can always add more controls if necessary.
That is why we say that a case-control design is an efficient sampling
from the underlying study population.
The case-control study includes only a couple of thousand women,
rather than all women in Boston.
Here, I described a very simple case-control design.
There are many variations of case-control designs,
which we cannot cover here.
But all of them have something in common.
As you may have already noticed, the case-control design
selects individuals based on their outcome.
Women who did develop cancer are much more likely to be included in the study
than women who did not develop cancer.
Therefore, our causal graph will include a note for selection--
C-- an arrow from the outcome Y to C, and a box around C
to indicate that the analysis is conditional on having been selected
into the study, which means that we are only one arrow away from selection
bias.
In case-control studies, we have to be especially careful,
because bias will occur any time that a selection also depends on the treatment
A. For example, suppose that convincing a random sample of women
without cancer to become our controls is too hard.
Rather, we decide that we're going to select most of our controls
among post-menopausal women who had a hip fracture
and are spending some time in the hospital.
This is definitely easier to do, because women with hip fracture are
in their beds and cannot run away from us when we ask them questions about
their use of hormone therapy A. So now, our tag includes the note,
hip fracture F, and an arrow from F to C.
And this is a serious problem.
Because hormone therapy A is known to have a causal effect on hip fracture.
It lowers the risk of hip fracture-- which
means that there is an arrow from A to F, and, therefore, selection bias.
A woman who used hormone therapy is less likely to have had a hip fracture,
and, therefore, less likely to have been selected as a control
than a woman who did not use hormone therapy.
Therefore, even if hormone therapy truly has a null effect on lung cancer,
in our case-control study, we will find an association between hormone therapy
and lung cancer.
Hormone therapy users have a greater probability
of being cases than controls.
So in case control studies, like in all designs with outcome-based selection,
an inappropriate selection of controls will introduce bias,
even when the true effect is null.
And this bias has different names in different settings.
For example, the bias is sometimes referred to
as "Berkson's bias" or "Berkson's fallacy,"
when the two diseases, Y and F, lead to hospitalization--
C-- and hospitalization is a selection criterion.
In summary, in studies with outcome-based selection,
beware of a strategy to select controls that may depend on
whether the controls were exposed to the treatment of interest.
