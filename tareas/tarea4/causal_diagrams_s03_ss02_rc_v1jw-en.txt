MIGUEL HERNAN: Selection bias is everywhere.
Every time we try to estimate the causal effect of certain treatment
or exposure, A, on an outcome, Y, we will be worried about selection bias.
Selection bias is feared when making inferences
from observational data, of course, but also
when conducting and analyzing randomized experiments.
And depending on the research setting, the bias receives different names.
For example, some common names for selection bias
are inappropriate selection of controls, Berkson's bias, loss to follow-up,
non-response bias, missing data bias, volunteer bias, self-selection bias,
healthy worker effect, et cetera.
All these biases will lead to incorrect estimates of causal effect.
And in all cases the bias is due to the process by which individuals or study
units are selected into the analysis.
Before we continue, there is something important we need to clarify.
Not all forms of selection create selection bias.
That is a big difference with confounding, the source of bias
that we discussed in the previous lesson.
Confounding, the presence of common causes of treatment and outcome,
always leads to bias, to incorrect estimates of causal effect.
In contrast, selection doesn't always lead to bias, to incorrect estimates.
For example, some forms of selection only
create bias if the treatment affects the outcome.
That is if there is an arrow from A to Y.
But these forms of selection do not create bias
if the treatment has a zero effect, a null effect on the outcome.
That is if there is no arrow from A to Y.
This means that if treatment has a non-null effect on Y,
the effect estimate will be incorrect.
But on the other hand, if the true effect of A and Y is null,
then the effect estimate will be correct, will be zero.
Here we're not going to consider those forms of selection.
Rather, we're going focus on the type of selection
that creates bias in all cases, whether the treatment has
a null or a non-null effect on the outcome.
When this second type of selection occurs,
the effect estimate will always be incorrect.
In fact, even if treatment A has no effect on Y,
we will find an association between A and Y.
So we will say that there is selection bias even under the null.
I didn't tell you, but in lesson 1, we already
discussed the structure of selection bias under the null.
The bias is the consequence of conditioning
on a common effect of treatment and outcome
or, as we will see, on a common effect of a cause of the treatment
and a cause of the outcome.
For example, in the causal diagram with arrows from treatment A and outcome
Y into C, conditioning on the selection node C, a collider,
will introduce bias even under the null.
In lesson 1, we saw a couple of examples of selection bias under the null.
Remember the example of the genetic and environmental determinants of cancer.
The bias arose because of conditioning or stratifying on a collider.
The collider was cancer, which is a common effect
of the genetic and environmental determinants.
Selecting a stratum of collider for the analysis
creates an association, a bias.
For this reason, selection bias under the null
is also referred to as collider stratification bias.
Now technically it is mathematically possible
that no bias will arise in one of the strata of the collider.
But here, we'll ignore that for simplicity.
